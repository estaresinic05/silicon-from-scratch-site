    Transistor Basics

    Transistors are the building blocks of every modern computer, and yet each one is nothing more than a switch. In this lesson we will look at how that switch actually works, and how just a handful of them, wired together, build the very logic gates we met earlier.

    The Anatomy of a Transistor

    Every transistor is built on a base of silicon called the substrate, drawn here in grey. Rising out of it is the part that actually does the work. Current enters at one end, called the source, and leaves at the other, called the drain. In the sketch these sit at the two ends of the green block. Between them lies the channel, the stretch of silicon that current has to cross to get from the source to the drain.

    Sitting over the channel is the gate, shown in red, and this is the piece that gives a transistor its power. The gate never actually touches the channel. A wafer-thin layer of insulating glass called the gate oxide, drawn in yellow, keeps the two apart, so the gate cannot pass any current of its own into the channel. Instead it controls the channel from a distance, purely through the electric field it creates when we place a voltage on it.

    This is the whole trick. When we put the right voltage on the gate, its electric field reaches through the oxide and pulls the channel into a conducting state, opening a path so that current flows freely from the source to the drain. Take the voltage away and the channel closes again, and no current can cross. The gate is the lever, and the source and drain are the two contacts it connects or separates. A transistor, for all its tiny complexity, really is just a switch that another wire gets to flip.
