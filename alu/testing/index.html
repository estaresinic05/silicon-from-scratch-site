    Testing Your ALU

    Our 32-bit ALU is complete — but a design isn't finished until it's been tested. We'll write a testbench: a block of Verilog that instantiates our ALU as the design under test and drives it with carefully chosen stimulus. By checking each operation's output against what we expect, we prove the design is correct before it ever becomes silicon.

    What Is a Testbench?

    So we just finished up our 32-bit ALU. It looks right on paper — but how will we actually know it's fully functional and bug-free? We could check a handful of cases by hand, but between all 32 bits of inputs a and b and the control code, we'd have to work through billions upon billions of possible input combinations before we could call our ALU bulletproof. So what should we do instead?

    One of the most crucial steps in the semiconductor design process is design verification. During this stage, a design's functional correctness is thoroughly tested — not by hand, but in simulation. Once we've developed a deep understanding of what our design is supposed to do, we can build a testbench to thoroughly exercise it and verify its behavior. You can picture a testbench as a wrapper that surrounds a module and aids with stimulus and automated checking.

    Testbenches come in all different shapes and sizes. You could have one written in Python that drives the design through a framework like cocotb, one in C that links against a compiled model of the hardware, or even one in Verilog that lives in the very same language as the design itself. Although each is slightly different, they all consist of one fundamental component: the Design Under Test, or DUT.

    The Design Under Test

    In our case, the DUT is the 32-bit ALU we just built. In the image provided, the inputs and outputs of our design act as the interface between the testbench and the DUT. Our inputs, a, b, and control, are given to the DUT from the testbench, and our outputs, result, ovf, and zero, are given to the testbench from the DUT.

    Before we can poke the ALU, we have to connect to it — and this is where everyone's first testbench trips up: which signals should be reg, and which should be wire?

    The rule follows who drives the signal. The testbench drives the ALU's inputs, so a, b, and control are declared reg — a reg can be assigned a value inside a procedural block, which is exactly what we'll do. The ALU drives its outputs back at us, so result, ovf, and zero are wire — we only observe them.

    With the signals declared, we instantiate the ALU — create one working copy of it — name that copy dut, and connect each of its ports to our signals.

    It helps to picture a real workbench: you clip a power supply onto the inputs to set them, and a multimeter onto the outputs to read them. reg is the power supply; wire is the multimeter. The #(.N(32)) passes the ALU's width as a parameter, so the very same testbench works for an ALU of any size.

    Applying Stimulus, Checking Automatically

    Everything happens inside an initial block — code that runs once, top to bottom, in simulated time. Testing one input combination is three steps: set the inputs, wait a moment for the logic to settle, then read the outputs back.

    That #(SETTLE) is a tiny delay. Our ALU is pure combinational logic with no clock, but its outputs still take a (simulated) instant to ripple through the gates — so we pause before trusting them.

    Now the leap that makes a testbench powerful. Instead of a human squinting at hexadecimal to decide whether it looks right, the testbench works out the expected answer itself — a golden model — and compares automatically. Ours re-derives each result with plain Verilog operators:

    Look closely at the overflow line: it uses a completely different formula than the ALU does. The ALU flags overflow by comparing the adder's carry-in and carry-out; the golden model instead inspects the sign bits directly. That independence is the whole point — if the checker simply copied the ALU's own logic, a bug in that logic would appear identically in both, and the test would happily pass. A good oracle is a second opinion, not an echo.

    With an expected answer in hand, the check is a single comparison. We use !== rather than != because !== is four-state: it also catches x (unknown) and z (floating) values, the garbage that means something has genuinely gone wrong.     Choosing Good Tests, and Reading Waveforms

    We still can't try every input — so we choose the ones most likely to break something. That's the idea of coverage: a small, curated set of vectors that each target a specific corner of the design.

    Read the note on each line and the reasoning shows through: ANDing two disjoint patterns should give exactly zero (does the zero flag fire?); addition is pushed one step past the largest positive and the smallest negative value to force overflow in both directions; subtracting equal operands is another zero; and set-less-than is handed the min/max pair — the case a naive comparison gets wrong unless overflow is handled. A handful of sharp vectors like these catch far more bugs than thousands of random ones.

    And when the run ends with RESULT: PASS, it means something concrete: on every vector we tried, our ALU agreed with an independent model of what it should do. That's real evidence the design is correct — not a hunch.
